# Research Brief: Gratitude

**Status: Approved 2026-11-11** (self-review: all six Phase 1 sections present; evidence graded; conflicts stated).

## 1. Clinical and experimental evidence

| Finding | Source | n / effect | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gratitude interventions reduce depression and anxiety symptoms slightly | Cregg & Cheavens 2021, *J Happiness Stud* meta-analysis | 27 studies, N=3,675; g=−0.29 post-test, −0.23 follow-up; smaller vs active controls | **Moderate** that the effect exists, and that it is **small** |
| Small gains in well-being; weak vs active controls | Davis et al. 2016, *J Couns Psychol* (≈32 samples); Dickens 2017 (38 studies) | Small effects vs measurement-only; near zero vs matched activities | **Moderate** for a small effect |
| Higher gratitude scores, better mental health, fewer anxiety and depression symptoms | Diniz et al. 2023, *Einstein* systematic review | 64 RCTs (33 pooled); GQ-6 +1.54 points; most trials high risk of bias | **Weak–moderate** |
| "Counting blessings" weekly improved well-being vs listing hassles | Emmons & McCullough 2003, *JPSP* | 3 studies, n≈65–192 each | **Moderate** (classic, but the comparison inflates the effect) |
| "Three good things" raised happiness for up to 6 months; the gratitude visit gave a large but short-lived boost (≈1 month) | Seligman et al. 2005, *Am Psychol* | n=577, internet sample | **Moderate** |
| Practising once a week beat three times a week | Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005 | Small study | **Weak** but influential: over-frequent practice goes stale |
| Imagining the absence of a good thing ("mental subtraction") lifts mood more than simply recalling it | Koo, Algoe, Wilson & Gilbert 2008, *JPSP* | 4 studies | **Moderate** |
| Gratitude practice before bed improved sleep quality and pre-sleep thoughts | Wood et al. 2009 (n=401, correlational); Jackowska et al. 2016 RCT (n=119) | Small–moderate | **Weak–moderate** |
| Gratitude delivered through hypnosis or guided audio | No controlled trials found | None | **No direct evidence** |

**Takeaway:** gratitude practice produces a real but small lift in mood and well-being. Specific, concrete, savoured content works better than generic lists. Novelty matters.

## 2. Large-sample user feedback
- Gratitude journaling apps (Gratitude, Five Minute Journal, Presently; hundreds of thousands of ratings, averages above 4.7★) are praised for being **short** (2–5 minutes), prompting a few specific items, and letting people look back over past entries. Looking back is repeatedly named as the most valued feature.
- Recurring complaints: repetitive prompts that feel like a chore after a few weeks, streak pressure and guilt, and paywalls on basic features.
- Guided gratitude meditations on Insight Timer and Calm (millions of plays): the most-played run 5–15 minutes. Listeners dislike long silences they weren't told about and instructions that feel forced ("just feel grateful").
- Category retention is low (Baumel 2019: median 30-day retention 3.3% across 93 mental health apps).
- **Limits:** high star averages reflect survivors; people who found gratitude practice hollow simply left. Expectancy is strong for a practice whose aim is "feel better".

## 3. Audio parameters

| Parameter | Choice | Evidence / reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Session length | ≈10 min | User data: 5–15 min guided sessions are played most; short enough to repeat a few times a week |
| Suggested frequency | 2–3 times a week, not daily | Lyubomirsky 2005 (weak): spacing prevents staleness. The app never shows streaks. |
| Entrainment | Binaural, isochronic fallback; supporting layer only | No gratitude-specific evidence; see `awake-entrainment.md` |
| Carrier | 220 Hz | Comfort, awake-module default |
| Beat timeline | 10 Hz (settling) → 7 Hz (imagery and savouring) → 10 Hz (integration) → 14 Hz (alert finish) | Alpha and light theta for relaxed imagery (weak); beta end for alertness (requirement) |
| Layers | Pink noise −16 dB, "glow" pad −22 dB, beats −34 dB | Warmer, brighter bed for a positive-affect session; preference only |
| Voice | Same soft voice as other hypnosis sessions, ~110 wpm | Consistency and user preference for a calm, unhurried voice |

## 4. Script design
- **Structure:** a brief settling induction (breath and body, light trance; no deep countdown, since this is a short awake practice) → three savouring exercises → integration suggestion → alert emergence.
- **Savouring exercises, in order of ease:**
  1. *One small good thing from today:* concrete and sensory (Seligman's "three good things", reduced to one deeply savoured item, since depth beats count).
  2. *A person:* someone who helped or cared for you; picture their face and silently thank them (the gratitude-visit effect, without the visit).
  3. *Mental subtraction:* briefly imagine life without one thing you rely on, then return to having it (Koo 2008).
- **Suggestion style:** permissive invitations ("see what comes to mind"; "it can be something very small"). Give silent time after each prompt, and announce the silence so it isn't mistaken for a glitch.
- **Post-hypnotic suggestion:** "over the coming days you may notice small good things a little more easily."
- **After the session:** an optional one-line note ("What came up?") stored in history, because users value looking back.
- **Avoid:** "you should feel grateful" or comparisons with people worse off (guilt-inducing, invalidating); forced positivity that dismisses real difficulty; gratitude towards people who harmed the listener; generic lists ("your health, your family") that assume circumstances.

## 5. Safety and contraindications
- Low risk. For people in depression, grief, or harmful relationships, gratitude prompts can feel invalidating or increase self-blame. The script explicitly says it's fine if nothing comes, and that gratitude doesn't mean pretending things are fine.
- A visible, low-key link to professional and crisis resources (the module touches mood).
- Standard cautions: not while driving; seizure caution for pulsed audio; volume limit.
- Ends fully alert.

## 6. Open questions and conflicts
- **Users vs research on dose:** users and apps push daily streaks; research hints that less frequent practice works better and that effects are small. *Resolution:* no streaks; the progress view shows mood change per session instead.
- **Audio vs writing:** nearly all evidence is for written exercises; guided audio is untested. *Resolution:* keep the evidence-based content (specific, savoured, person-directed, subtraction) and add the optional written note.
- **Effect size honesty:** benefits are small and partly expectancy. The app describes this as a mood-supporting practice, not a treatment.
